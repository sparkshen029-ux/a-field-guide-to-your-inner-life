# Introduction

[← Back to README](../README.md) · [Next →](01-the-inner-life-is-real.md)

There are good guides to staying alive. In the past few years, someone assembled six hundred and sixty-five recommendations on how not to die early, not to lose money, not to sign away your freedom: graded by how strong the evidence is, each one with sources. If you have not seen it, look up *HowToLiveBetter*. I keep it within reach and I have done maybe a tenth of it.

What nobody has written, or nobody has written this way, is a guide to the other half. The half where you lie awake at 3 a.m. replaying an argument. The half that produces the same dream four times, or a sudden dislike of someone you just met, or the tightness in your chest on Sunday evening. Guides to the outer life tell you what to do about your body, your money, your paperwork. Your inner life gets apps and slogans, or silence.

This little guide is for that half. It comes from somewhere specific. I teach psychology in China. The tradition I trained in is called **imagery dialogue** (*yixiang duìhuà*), founded by the psychologist Zhu Jianjun. It works with dreams and with mental images: the pictures that show up in your head when feeling takes shape. Zhu's main books have never been translated into English. Most of what the English-speaking world knows about Chinese psychology could fit in a pamphlet, and most of that pamphlet would be about ancient philosophy. Meanwhile a working tradition, with clinics, with training programs, with thirty years of cases, has been developing on the other side of a language wall.

I cannot carry the whole tradition across that wall. I am not going to pretend I am Zhu Jianjun; I am not, and this guide is not his voice. It is my attempt to translate a few of its most useful ideas into plain English, mixed, where I can, with findings from Western research that point the same way. Where the two disagree, I will tell you. Where I am guessing, I will tell you that too.

How to use it: each section stands alone. Each ends with an *Honesty note* that separates what the tradition says, what research supports, and what is just my own read. Pick one entry. Try it once. That counts. I have done perhaps a tenth of this book too, and I wrote it.

One thing it is not: this is not therapy, and it is not a substitute for it. There is a section near the end about where self-help stops working. Go straight to it if you need it. The rest will keep.
