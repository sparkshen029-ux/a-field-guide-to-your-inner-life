### 4. Don't dam it, don't dive in: slow the body first

Two ways of handling anger, and both are popular, and both fail.

The first is damming. Press it down, be reasonable, swallow it. Zhu Jianjun's image for this comes from his lectures: a dam. The water keeps coming, the dam gets higher, and the structure gets less safe every year, until one day it fails at a load it was never rated for, usually over something small, which is why everyone in the room is confused about where the rage came from.

The second is diving. Scream, punch the pillow, smash the plates, get it *out*. The old theory behind this says pressure releases. The evidence came in the other way: venting keeps the body's alarm system lit. People who act angry get better at being angry, the way people who practice anything get better at it.

The third way is unglamorous: slow the body down and let the feeling be there without feeding it. Leave the room. Not to punish anyone, just because a body takes a few minutes to come down from full alarm, and no useful sentence was ever composed inside two of those minutes. When your chest is hammering, that is not you; that is your pulse having an opinion. Wait until it lowers its voice.

Then, later, this is the part people skip, look at what the anger was standing in front of. Usually something smaller and more honest: a boundary nobody had said out loud, a tiredness nobody had admitted.

> **Honesty note.** *The tradition:* the dam image for suppression and the warning against both stuffing and flooding are standard teaching in this tradition's lectures. *The research:* venting's failure to discharge anger and the value of lowering physiological arousal are supported (see *HowToLiveBetter*, §3 #23, with sources); anger-management programs built on arousal regulation and skill training review as effective in the offender-treatment literature (Redondo & Andrés-Pueyo, 2007). *My own experience:* I can confirm that punching things is satisfying and that satisfaction is not the same as improvement, from both sides of that sentence.

### 5. Let sadness finish its work

There is a modern habit of treating sadness as a malfunction with a five-minute fix. Dance it away, reframe it, gratitude-journal it into remission. Some of this is fine. Some of it teaches people that grief is a problem of attitude.

Here is what the research actually shows, and it is kinder than the hustle. Most people, after most losses, heal on their own. Not evenly, not on schedule, but on their own; the capacity is common, far more common than the popular story of fragility suggests. The resilience researcher Ann Masten found that children who bounce back from serious adversity are not rare exceptions but "more numerous than imagined." And interventions offered to *everyone* who grieves, well-meaning and professional, show small benefits that fade, precisely because most of the people in them were going to be fine and the support interfered with nothing and helped with little.

Which means the strange, useful advice is: at first, mostly, let sadness work. It has a job. Sleep gets strange; appetite gets strange; the world gets two sizes too large. None of this is instantly a problem. Sadness is how a person goes around the hole where something used to be, day after day, until the route stops being a detour and becomes just the way you walk.

What it does not mean: that suffering is noble, or that you should isolate. Sadness with company heals better than sadness alone. And if months pass and it isn't working, see entry 23, which is about exactly that.

> **Honesty note.** *The tradition:* this tradition has less to say about ordinary grief than about stuck grief; it agrees with the mainstream that feeling must be felt to be processed. *The research:* resilience after loss is common (Masten, 2014, via Bartol's textbook; Bonanno's work reviewed in *HowToLiveBetter* §29 #9, with sources); universal grief interventions underperform; targeted ones work. *My own experience:* the people I have sat with while they grieved did not need my advice. They needed a chair and someone who didn't flinch.

### 6. Ask your anxiety what it is guarding

Anxiety is the emotion everyone wants evicted, so try this reframe and see if it survives the week: anxiety is a guard. An overworked, under-briefed guard, but a guard. It patrols, it wakes you at night, it makes a racket when something gets too close to the gate.

The question that unlocks it is not "how do I stop this?" It's "*what are you guarding?*" Ask it slowly, and the answers are rarely what the panic suggests. Sometimes it's a relationship being carried too far past honesty. Sometimes it's a work situation that needs one honest sentence. Sometimes it's a body that needs a doctor and has been asking in the only language it has left.

The tradition's habit of turning a feeling into a figure, giving the anxiety a shape, and letting that shape be a person or animal, is not decoration. A vague dread cannot answer questions. A guard can. When you imagine it with a body, two things become possible: you can ask what it's protecting, and you can notice how *tired* it looks. People are often moved by this. The thing ruining their sleep turns out to be working night shifts for them, unpaid, on a promise nobody made.

Do not confuse any of this with giving in. A guard that's guarding nothing real, a door that locked years ago, can be told so. But you audit the guard before you fire it. That's the whole method: curiosity first, change later, in that order.

> **Honesty note.** *The tradition:* personifying emotion and dialoguing with it is core imagery-dialogue practice; the guard is my image, the dialogue is not. *The research:* I will not dress this one up: the specific claim that dialoguing with personified anxiety is effective has no direct trial I can point you to. Aromatherapy-level confidence should not be granted. *My own experience:* it works often enough, and fails interestingly, the failures usually being people who asked the question already knowing the answer and didn't want to hear it.

### 7. Other people's highlight reels are not evidence about you

You know this already, and you do it anyway, which is the interesting part. You see a college roommate's promotion, a stranger's kitchen, a friend's child's violin recital, and something in you does instant, bad accounting: *their* life is a parade of finished meals; *yours* is a pile of laundry. The comparison is rigged in three ways at once: you're comparing your Tuesday to their best frame, their outside to your inside, and their edited reel to your full footage.

None of this is a personal failure. It's a design exploit. These platforms didn't invent envy; they industrialized it.

The tradition's read of envy is less moralistic than the usual one: envy is a compass. It points, with embarrassing accuracy, at what you actually want and are not admitting you want. The colleague whose success makes your teeth itch is usually carrying your abandoned plan. You don't have to act on the compass. But sneering at it ("I'm not jealous, I just think it's tacky") wastes the only honest data in the room.

Practically, two moves. First, the mundane one, with evidence behind it: cut the feed. Curate like your mood depends on it, because it does. Second, the older one: next time the itch arrives, ask what it points at. Then either go get that thing, or grieve it honestly, or decide you don't want it after all; all three are respectable. Doom-scrolling about it is the only option with no return.

> **Honesty note.** *The tradition:* envy as a pointer to disowned desire is a recurring move in this tradition's reading of feeling. *The research:* social comparison on social platforms correlates with worse mood in multiple studies; limiting use is a supported, cheap intervention (see *HowToLiveBetter* §3 #20, with sources). The "compass" claim specifically is the tradition's, not a trial result. *My own experience:* my own compass once pointed, embarrassingly, at someone who simply worked fewer hours than me. The data was correct. I did something about it. It took two more years, which the compass had declined to mention.

### 26. The mall is not a mood repair shop

It starts honestly: buying something does lift your mood for a while. The package arrives, the day has a small peak in it. The trouble is what the habit teaches. If the circuit that runs "bad feeling, then buy something" fires often enough, it becomes the default answer, and you have assigned your emotional regulation to a checkout button.

The research here is more careful than the pop version, so let me be careful too. A meta-analysis of 259 samples found that the more people center money and possessions, the lower their reported well-being, but the correlation is weak (around r = −0.19), and nearly all of it is simultaneous snapshots that cannot say which causes which. The honest reading is not "shopping makes you unhappy." It is "counting on shopping to make you happy is a thin plan." The stronger finding points somewhere else entirely: across surveys and experiments, people reliably got more lasting happiness from money spent on *experiences* (a trip, a meal with someone) than from money spent on objects.

The tradition I trained in would put it this way: the feeling is asking a question, and the purchase answers a different one. The tightness in your chest on Tuesday night is usually about something: a conversation you're avoiding, a week with no rest in it, a loneliness with a specific shape. A package answers none of those. What actually has evidence behind it for a low mood is unglamorous: movement, daylight, sleep, a person, and the section near the end of this book if it goes deep.

None of this is an argument against owning things you love. Buy the coat. Just know which purchases are for the body and which are an invoice the mood sent to the wrong address.

> **Honesty note.** *The tradition:* this school treats mood-driven spending as a feeling answering with a symbol instead of a sentence; that framing is clinical habit, not doctrine. *The research:* materialism and well-being, Dittmar et al., 2014 meta-analysis ([doi:10.1037/a0037409](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037409)), weak and mostly cross-sectional, as stated above; experiences versus things, Van Boven & Gilovich, 2003 ([doi:10.1037/0022-3514.85.6.1193](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.85.6.1193)); both summarized with sources in *HowToLiveBetter* §6 #23. *My own experience:* my own worst purchases have all been made after 10 p.m., which is also when, as entry 22 explains, my judgment is running on the least sleep and the most suggestion.

### 27. Put "everyone demands I be perfect" in the symptom column, not the fact column

Perfectionism is not one thing, and the two versions are not twins. One is *I set high standards for myself.* The other is *the people around me demand perfection, and anything less means I'm finished.* The second one, psychologists call it socially prescribed perfectionism, is the dangerous one, and the reason is that it isn't a standard. It's a belief about other minds, usually a false one, running as an operating system.

Pooled across 45 studies and nearly 12,000 people, externally pressured perfectionism correlates with suicidal thinking at around r = .28, nearly three times stronger than self-set perfectionism (r = .10), which behaves like a plain character trait, not a wound. Follow-up studies find the same ordering. The tradition I trained in has a phrase for what to do with a belief like this, and it is the title of this entry: put it in the symptom column. A symptom is something you observe and treat; a fact is something you obey. "My boss expects flawlessness" feels like a fact the way a fever feels like a normal temperature. Both are the body (or mind) reporting from a skewed instrument.

This is also the inner critic of entry 17 wearing its public face. The critic's voice claims to speak for everyone. One cheap, effective experiment: actually ask. Not rhetorically: literally ask one real person what they expect of you, and write the answer down. The gap between the written answer and the voice in your head is the size of the malfunction.

To be clear about what this entry does not say: it does not say lower your standards. High standards are fine and often lovely. The problem is never the bar. The problem is the imaginary tribunal that was never convened.

> **Honesty note.** *The tradition:* treating harsh inner demands as a symptom to examine rather than a fact to obey is standard practice in this school, and it connects directly to the inner-critic work of entry 17. *The research:* Smith et al., 2018, meta-analysis of perfectionism and suicide ([doi:10.1111/jopy.12333](https://doi.org/10.1111/jopy.12333)); correlations are moderate, mostly cross-sectional, and the authors note possible publication bias in one subgroup; the direction is consistent but this is not a destiny number; if such thoughts turn toward ending your life, entry 24 comes first. *My own experience:* every time I have actually asked the imagined tribunal what it wants, the real humans wanted less than half of what the voice claimed. The voice has never once been consulted about this.

### 28. When the whirlpool pretends to be a river

Replaying the same painful scene, the argument, the interview, the thing you said in 2019, feels like work. It has the posture of work: serious, repetitive, frowning. The tradition I trained in has an image for what it actually is, from the lectures: a whirlpool. Feeling spins, catches another feeling, spins it too, until the whole thing turns in a circle and calls itself thinking.

The research agrees the water isn't going anywhere. Rumination, the technical name for chewing the same content without moving, deepens depression, strengthens negative thinking, weakens actual problem-solving, gets in the way of doing anything, and slowly exhausts the people around you who keep getting the same call. Notice that list: it damages exactly the faculties you would need to resolve the thing you're ruminating about. The whirlpool doesn't just fail to be a river. It digs the banks higher.

The countermove is almost embarrassingly physical: when you catch yourself circling, switch to a task that uses your hands. Wash the dishes. Fix the hinge. Pull weeds. The point is not distraction as avoidance; the thing itself still needs facing, later, with words. The point is that a circling mind cannot be argued out of its circle from inside. It has to be walked out through the body, and then, on flat ground, you can decide what the scene actually asks of you.

How do you tell the whirlpool from real reflection before you're soaked? River thinking moves: it produces a new sentence, a next step, sometimes an exit. Whirlpool thinking produces the same three sentences with worse lighting each pass. If an hour of "thinking it through" has generated nothing you could write on a card, you weren't thinking. You were circling. Hands, then words.

> **Honesty note.** *The tradition:* the whirlpool image for feelings feeding on each other in a loop comes from this school's lectures, and its standing correction is the same as the research's: move, then face the matter. *The research:* Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco & Lyubomirsky, 2008, "Rethinking Rumination" ([doi:10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x)); it establishes the harms and endorses attention-shifting, but the review is directional: no number, and no controlled comparison of *which* replacement activity works best. "Hands-busy tasks" is craft, flagged as such. *My own experience:* my own whirlpool's favorite hours are 10 to midnight, which tells you everything, since entry 22 already knows what my worst hours are made of.

### 29. Stress is a load, not a mood

We talk about stress as if it were a feeling. It is closer to a load: something the body carries, with a physics you can describe. The classic account, worked out by Hans Selye last century and still the backbone of every stress physiology course, runs in three stages. Alarm: the body floods, heart and breath go up, this is the fight-or-flight surge. Resistance: the load keeps coming, so the body stays mobilized and pays for it; this is the stage where sleep frays, periods can stop, testosterone drops, and small illnesses move in like tenants. Exhaustion: the system runs out of adjustments, and things break.

Two details from that literature deserve more fame than they get. First, the load does not have to be bad news. Job promotions, marriages, newborns: positive life changes appear on the standard stressor lists, because what stresses the body is *adjustment*, not misfortune. This explains a mystery many people meet directly: why a good year can leave you strangely flattened. Second, in the interrogation research that entry 22 borrows from, the catalog of what breaks people is brutally mundane: isolation, fatigue, hunger, sleep loss, pain, threat. Not torture in the movie sense, just those, stacked. Most readers' lives contain a politely stacked version of the same list, minus the threat. The body reads it the same way; only the invoice is smaller.

What follows for daily life is unromantic. When the load is heavy, the first thing to cut is not the errand you enjoy but the pretense that you can carry it all at baseline sleep. And when a good life change lands, treat it as what it is: a load. Rest is not something you earn back after the wedding, the move, the new job. It is part of the carrying.

> **Honesty note.** *The tradition:* this school's teaching is that imagery work rests on the body's state; the load framing is its natural vocabulary, though the three stages themselves belong to physiology, not to the tradition. *The research:* Selye's general adaptation syndrome as taught in standard abnormal-psychology texts (the Chinese textbook on our shelf: Qian Mingyi's, with the fight-or-flight and GAS stages at pp. 411–413); the stressor catalog and its consequences appear throughout Gudjonsson's handbook on interrogation psychology, the same source entry 22 draws on. *My own experience:* I once graded forty exam scripts in two days of ordinary good news and then wondered why I was sick for a week. The body does not read the news. It reads the load.
