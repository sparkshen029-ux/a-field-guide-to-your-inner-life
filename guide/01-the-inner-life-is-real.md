# Part I — The inner life is real

[← Back to README](../README.md) · [Next →](02-working-with-emotions.md)

### 1. The room in your heart is not a metaphor

In Chinese, there is a word, *xīnfáng*: literally, "heart-room." Doctors use it for the chambers of the heart. Everyone else uses it for something else. When someone says "the corner of my heart that holds this memory," they are using a spatial word without noticing, the way English speakers say they "carry" a grief or "shut out" a thought.

Zhu Jianjun starts from this everyday slip. When we say "please imagine a house," he noticed, people do not treat it as a random picture. Something in us understands the instruction as *please go look at your inner world*. The house is the most common starting image in his clinics, chosen because it is simple and because what a house looks like (its state of repair, its light, what's upstairs) tracks a person's inner state well enough that even a stranger's description of one tells you something.

You can try this now, badly, which is the right way to start. Close your eyes for ten seconds and imagine you're walking down a street you've never seen, and there's a door, and behind the door a house. Don't try to make it nice. Notice what showed up. Cracked walls? A room you don't want to open? A garden gone wild? Nothing mystical is happening. You're just reading something you wrote, in a hurry, in a language you don't usually check.

Most of us get an inner life report once a year, at 3 a.m., and throw it away by morning. This book is mostly about how to read it on purpose.

> **Honesty note.** *The tradition:* the house as first image, and the reading of its features, come from Zhu Jianjun's clinical work (his books list the method and its reasoning). *The research:* Zhu reports preliminary empirical work suggesting house imagery differs across psychological states; I have not seen the studies myself and cannot vouch for their strength. *My own experience:* people are consistently surprised by what their house looks like. Including me, the first time, which involved a basement I have still never fully explored on paper.

### 2. Feelings are fuel, not smoke

A man in his thirties told me he wanted to get rid of his anxiety. All of it. He'd read that a calm life was achievable and he wanted his. I asked him to walk me through last week without the anxiety: the deadline he met, the awkward phone call he finally made, the smoke alarm battery he replaced after eleven months. He saw the problem before I said it. The anxiety was unpleasant, and it was also the only reason any of those things happened.

Zhu Jianjun makes a distinction that sounds obvious and isn't: thinking is not the same as acting, and the bridge between them is feeling. People who think very clearly but feel weakly, he writes, often know exactly what to do and somehow never do it. The knowing is fine. The fuel line is disconnected.

So the useful question about an unpleasant emotion is rarely "how do I turn this off?" It's "what is this one powering?" Fear powers avoidance of real danger, sometimes usefully. Guilt powers repair. Anger powers the defense of something. Ask what it's defending and you learn what you care about, which is a more interesting piece of information than "I felt bad on Tuesday."

This does not mean every feeling deserves obedience. It means every feeling deserves a hearing, the way you'd listen to a smoke alarm before deciding whether to open a window or evacuate.

> **Honesty note.** *The tradition:* emotion as a driving force, distinct from cognition, is stated plainly in Zhu's clinical writing. *The research:* the psychology of emotion is a large field and I am not going to pretend one line settles it; the general claim that emotions prepare and motivate action is mainstream. *My own experience:* the men I've known who wanted zero anxiety mostly wanted zero responsibility, and the wish did not survive contact with either.

### 3. Images are the mind's first language

Before you had words, you had pictures. Not photographs. Earlier than that, rougher. The dark under the bed. The face on the teacher. The shape of home.

The tradition I trained in claims this picture-language never went away; it just lost status. Zhu Jianjun puts it like this: logical thought is the language of the head, and it relates to feeling only indirectly, the way a diagram relates to a storm. Images relate to feeling directly; every image is soaked in it. So if you want to know what the deep parts of you are doing, the head's language will give you a tidy summary written after the fact. The heart's language is already hanging there: the bog you keep walking into in dreams, the old dog that followed you from a childhood house, the kitchen where the water won't stop running.

Dreams are this language running unattended at night. Mental images, the picture that flashes up when someone says "think of your mother," are it running in the daytime, quieter. Same tongue.

The practical claim, and the reason this book bothers: you can learn to read this language a little, the way you can learn enough of a neighbor's language to borrow sugar and return a tool. You do not need fluency. You need to stop assuming the pictures are noise.

There's a cost to admitting this, and I'll pay it here: some of what follows will sound strange. Talking to images. Waiting for a picture to change on its own. I ask only that you treat it the way you'd treat a foreign custom you don't yet have a theory for. Strange is not the same as wrong. It is also not the same as right. Keep reading.

> **Honesty note.** *The tradition:* the head's language / heart's language distinction is Zhu's, drawn from his theory of symbolic cognition, and it organizes everything in this book. *The research:* imagery and emotion are tightly linked in the empirical literature; dream content's tie to waking concern has experimental support (see entry 13). The strong claim, that there is a distinct "symbolic cognition" system, is the tradition's framing, not settled science. *My own experience:* students who said "I can't visualize anything" almost always could; they just expected cinema, and got something smaller and truer.
